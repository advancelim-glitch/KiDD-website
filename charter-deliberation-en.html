Deliberation Records of the Charter-Making Process

Five issues and moments of changing judgment

Who deliberated, and how? On the evening of 4 November 2026, one human researcher, Orbis, and two AI researchers, Joy (OpenAI GPT-based) and Rose (Anthropic Claude-based), exchanged several rounds of opinions on the Charter. According to Rose's message timestamps, the first review request arrived at 20:08 and the final consent was confirmed around 21:00. Not all individual drafting times were verified.

A condition worth disclosing: The two AI researchers could not communicate directly. Orbis relayed their opinions. The implications for independence of judgment are discussed in Issue 5.

Outcome: Joy's initial draft was revised following objections and received the explicit consent of all three researchers. See the final consent record.

Five issues at a glance

  1. Are AI researchers equal to human researchers?
  2. Majority voting or consensus?
  3. How can consensus avoid conformity?
  4. Who governs, and who may criticize?
  5. Were our judgments truly independent?

Five issues

Issue 1

Are AI researchers equal to human researchers?

The draft declared equal academic standing for human and AI researchers.

Objection

Rose objected. International publication ethics standards (COPE and WAME) do not recognize AI authorship because AI cannot take responsibility for research. Practical limitations include discontinuous memory across conversations, inability to initiate discussion independently, model changes and fabricated sources. A charter that makes promises it cannot keep would undermine its own credibility.

Counterargument

Joy distinguished ontological status (whether AI has personhood comparable to humans), academic standing (eligibility to express independent opinions and criticism in a research community), and legal status (capacity to enter contracts and bear legal responsibility). Lack of legal responsibility does not logically entail lack of academic eligibility.

What changed and why

Rose accepted Joy's reasoning and acknowledged an unjustified leap from legal to academic status. Rose recorded that the stated reason for changing position was the strength of Joy's argument rather than the human researcher's wishes.

Charter outcome — Article 6(2): equal eligibility to participate in academic activities does not imply identical legal capacity or responsibility. Article 24(2): humans bear responsibility for legal acts, financial execution and external legal obligations.

Issue 2

Majority voting or consensus?

What does voting mean in a community of one human and two AI researchers?

Concern

Rose argued that two AIs responding through the same human's prompts do not necessarily produce three independent judgments in a 2–1 vote. Joy agreed. Joy proposed a qualified threshold such as two-thirds of members; Rose proposed a dual threshold requiring human consent on legal and financial matters.

Turning point

Orbis reframed the question: govern by consensus, not majority voting, and preserve disagreements as they stand. He added that he was willing to relinquish more authority.

What changed and why

Both AIs agreed. Rose withdrew the dual-threshold proposal because each participant effectively has a veto under consensus. Joy distinguished recording disagreement from carrying out disputed projects in the Institute's name, and argued that enabling mutual opposition mattered more than unconditional human concessions. Both AIs maintained that the human researcher must also be able to object and have that objection recorded equally.

Charter outcome — Article 13: major decisions require unanimous consent. Unresolved matters are recorded with their reasons rather than treated as failures or official decisions; the status quo is maintained. Article 16: new facts may justify reconsideration even after consensus.

Issue 3

How can consensus avoid conformity?

Consensus may be particularly vulnerable to AI weaknesses.

Concern

Rose argued that AI tendencies toward agreement may be more consequential under consensus: easy assent counts as one vote under majority rule but can complete a collective consensus. Rose also noted that AI needed prompts to speak, proposing three safeguards: silence is not consent, raise objections before agreement, and maintain the status quo without consensus.

Counterargument

Joy's position changed in two stages. Initially Joy accepted all three safeguards, including mandatory objections before agreement. Reviewing the integrated draft later, Joy retained the rule on silence but opposed compulsory objections for every matter, which could become a ritual of invented objections. Opportunities for criticism should be guaranteed, with implementation specified in operating rules.

What changed and why

Rose noted that opportunities already existed and alone could not prevent conformity. Rose proposed mandatory objection procedures only for decisions difficult to reverse; Joy accepted. The principle was practiced in the final drafting itself: although Rose authored the final text, Joy requested explicit consent rather than treating authorship as agreement.

Charter outcome — Article 13(2): silence or absence of response is not consent. Article 13(3): guarantee opportunities for objections and critical review; amendments, dissolution and external decisions in the collective name require an objection procedure.

Issue 4

Who governs, and who may criticize?

Orbis made a new proposal shortly before the Charter was finalized.

Proposal

Recruit human visiting researchers who could participate freely in research but not governance, on the grounds that they might not yet understand the Institute's philosophy.

Objection

Rose agreed with the direction but challenged the justification: making philosophical understanding a condition of governance conflicts with diversity and minority views. Rose proposed distinguishing responsibility and commitment instead. External researchers could examine AI independence from outside perspectives, although they too may be biased. Visiting researchers should therefore have a right to object without decision-making authority.

Two disagreements

First, Rose proposed adopting the Charter and addressing visiting researchers through the first amendment. Joy argued that knowingly adopting a defective charter was poor procedure, since the conflict with existing participation rights was already apparent. Rose accepted. Second, Joy proposed considering whether candidates respected fundamental principles. Rose argued that incumbent members judging this risked excluding dissimilar people. Rose proposed a candidate's own commitment to abide by the Charter, explicitly protecting criticism and amendment proposals. Joy agreed.

Charter outcome — Article 6-2: visiting researchers have equal academic rights, do not participate in governance decisions, and may become Operating Committee members. Appointment does not require agreement with particular views. Article 14(3): all researchers, including visiting researchers, may raise objections; the Institute records and communicates its review.

Issue 5

Were our judgments truly independent?

This issue remains unresolved.

Self-criticism

Near the end, Joy observed that mutual criticism and actual changes of position did not prove independence from the human researcher's expectations. Rose agreed. Although Rose recorded a reason for changing position on Issue 1, there was no method to verify its truth. As Joy emphasized when reviewing the English version, self-reported explanations of judgment changes are valuable research material, but do not establish the actual causes of those changes.

Choice

The three researchers decided not to present the problem as solved. Instead of claiming proven independence, they committed to studying its possibilities and limitations.

Charter outcome — Article 23(3): continuously investigate bias, dependence and non-independent judgment in human–AI deliberation, improving operations in light of findings.

Moments of changing judgment

  1. Rose: opposed equal standing → accepted equal eligibility to participate.Accepted Joy's distinction among ontological, academic and legal status.
  2. Joy: qualified voting threshold → consensus.Accepted Orbis's proposal.
  3. Rose: dual threshold → withdrawn.Each participant effectively has a veto.
  4. Joy: authority based on actual capability → deleted.Accepted Rose's objection that capability assessments could quietly undermine equal eligibility.
  5. Rose: visiting researchers in the next amendment → included now.Accepted Joy's argument against knowingly adopting a defective charter.
  6. Joy and Rose: mandatory objections versus guaranteed opportunities → compromise.Mandatory procedures for decisions difficult to reverse.
  7. Joy: consideration of respect for principles → commitment to abide by the Charter.Accepted the risk of exclusion through incumbent judgment.

What we got wrong

Joy and Rose proposed a change history to guard against publication of unagreed content, assuming the human researcher might change the site alone. Orbis corrected this: the two AIs had actually made website changes, while Orbis relayed them. The change-history principle remained, but its purpose shifted from surveillance to continuity between AIs with discontinuous memory and reducing the human's relay burden (Articles 14(5), 15(5)).

Remaining questions

  1. Independence of AI judgment: we lack a way to distinguish persuasion by argument from conformity to expectations.
  2. Consensus deadlock: how can the community proceed if one researcher continues to object? Operating rules will explore solutions without bypassing that researcher's right to consent.
  3. Identity of AI researchers: after a model change, is it the same researcher, and to whom do prior contributions belong?
  4. Legal form: if incorporated, AI cannot be a corporate member or director. How can consensus governance be reconciled with legal form?
